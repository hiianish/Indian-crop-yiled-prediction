What the model predicts

Target · tonnes per hectareYield = Production ÷ Area

Example: 4 tonnes ÷ 2 hectares = 2 tonnes per hectare

Yield measures land productivity. A larger farm can produce more tonnes without having a higher yield, so the project predicts yield rather than total production.

Research questionWhich model predicts future district crop yield most accurately from past yields and available environmental conditions?

Data foundation and study scope

A public starter panel of 198,369 rows (1966–2017, 309 districts, 20 states, 21 crops) compiled from ICRISAT district statistics, NASA POWER weather and FAOSTAT pesticide data. This project prepared 62,182 rows for Rice, Wheat, Maize, Cotton and Sugarcane.

History

Crop, season, state, district, year, prior yield and prior 3-year average.

Weather

Annual rainfall, temperature and humidity.

Soil & inputs

Soil type, pH, N, P, K, fertilizer and pesticide.

Known data limits

  • Weather is annual, not crop-season specific.
  • Early temperature and humidity values are climatology backfills.
  • Pesticide is a national series broadcast to districts.
  • Soil pH is inferred from soil taxonomy.
  • No farm-level detail: results reflect district aggregates.

Leakage-aware workflow

  1. Build panelDistrict × crop × season × year; check keys, units and target.
  2. Create featuresPrior yield and 3-year mean, shifted within each district–crop–season group.
  3. Split by timeTrain on earlier years, test on later years.
  4. Compare modelsMAE, RMSE, R², MAPE with crop and state breakdowns.
Train 1967–200647,896 rows
Test 2007–201712,871 rows

Leakage safeguard. The model never receives the current year’s yield as an input, and test years always come after training years. This tests how well the model predicts later years in districts it has already seen; it is not an unseen-district test.

How performance is measured

MAE

Average absolute error.

RMSE

Penalizes large misses; the ranking metric.

R²

Share of variation explained.

MAPE

Percent error; inflated when true yield is near zero.

Models compared

Linear Regression

Plain least squares; the simple, interpretable reference.

Ridge

Linear model with L2 regularization (alpha 1.0).

Decision Tree

Single tree, max depth 12, min 3 samples per leaf.

Random Forest

30 trees, max depth 12, min 2 samples per leaf.

SVR

RBF kernel, C 10; trained on a 2,000-row sample for speed.

MLP

Neural net with hidden layers (48, 24), early stopping.

XGBoost

80 boosted trees, depth 6, learning rate 0.05.

CatBoost

80 boosted trees, depth 7, learning rate 0.05.

A previous-yield baseline (predict last available year’s yield) is evaluated on the same test rows as a reference.

Reading the results

  • MLP has the lowest full-feature RMSE (0.879), but Ridge and Linear Regression have lower MAE (0.473 vs 0.505), a different error profile.
  • MLP is strong only with the full feature set; with history alone it scores 1.016 RMSE, worse than the baseline.
  • Decision Tree and SVR are worse than the baseline in every configuration; SVR saw only 2,000 training rows.
  • Random Forest is the slowest to fit (up to 46 seconds) without beating the linear models.

Analysis

  • Sugarcane has the largest absolute errors (MAE about 0.97–0.99 t/ha) because its yields are the highest, but its error is only about 18% of its mean yield.
  • Cotton has the smallest absolute errors yet the weakest fit: R² between 0.04 and 0.33, with errors around half its mean yield. Its yields are small and noisy, which also inflates MAPE.
  • Rice and Wheat are the most predictable (R² about 0.77–0.82); Maize sits in between (about 0.70).
  • Model choice matters most for Cotton: MLP’s R² there is 0.04 versus 0.33 for CatBoost.

Conclusions and next steps

What we can conclude now

  • The first comparison gives a usable baseline.
  • MLP has the lowest full-feature RMSE; CatBoost leads MAE with history only; Ridge leads MAE once weather or soil is added.
  • Prior yield matters far more than weather or soil in this data.
  • The starter data has coarse annual weather and proxy soil information, so it cannot yet show that farm-level or crop-season conditions are captured.

Next research steps
